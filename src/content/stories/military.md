The museum’s military exhibit honors the men and women of the Melrose area who served, from the Civil War forward. Uniforms, medals, caps, patches, photographs and keepsakes fill the cases, and each war has a short note on the exhibit that sets the scene.

**Wars shown in the exhibit**

- **Spanish-American War (1898).** Cuban rebels had sought independence from Spain since the 1880s. The U.S. stayed officially neutral until the U.S.S. Maine was sunk in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898. The war ended in Spanish defeat that August. Cuba became independent, and the U.S. received Puerto Rico, the Philippines and Guam.
- **World War I (1914–1918).** The assassination of Archduke Ferdinand in 1914 set off a war across most of Europe. The U.S. entered on April 6, 1917, on the side of the Allies, and the armistice came on November 11, 1918.
- **World War II (1939–1945).** The U.S. remained neutral until Japan bombed Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. Germany surrendered on May 8, 1945, and Japan on August 14.
- **Korean War (1950–1953).** Korea was divided in 1945 into a Soviet-occupied North and a U.S.-occupied South. Fighting grew into full war by 1950, with U.N. forces aiding the South and Chinese forces the North. Peace terms were agreed in 1953.
- **Vietnam War (1965–1975).** U.S. troops were sent to South Vietnam early in 1965. After the Tet offensive of 1968, troop withdrawals began in 1969 and a cease-fire was agreed in 1973. North Vietnam united the country in April 1975.
- **Persian Gulf War (1990–1991).** Iraq invaded Kuwait on August 2, 1990. The U.S. joined 34 countries in Operation Desert Storm, which began January 16, 1991. Iraq withdrew on February 28, 1991.

### Two Melrose-area heroes

Two local airmen from different wars have their own displays.

### Capt. James Gallagher

On Feb. 26, 1949, Lucky Lady, a Boeing B-50 Superfortress bomber piloted by Melrose native Captain James Gallagher, took off from Carswell Air Force Base, Fort Worth, TX. It carried a large cargo of food, bedding, and toiletries for the 14 crewmen who would take the plane on its 4-day trip. The only thing Lucky Lady couldn’t carry was enough fuel to keep it airborne for the whole trip. It was refueled in mid-air several times, with highly flammable high-octane fuel. Total air time was 78 hours 54 minutes; average 249 miles per hour. When the news broke of this first successful non-stop circumnavigation of the globe, it made the front page of the Minneapolis Tribune, which also ran a series of comic strips about the adventure.

The celebration of Capt. Gallagher’s triumph started with a caravan of cars which left Minneapolis on May 20 (coincidentally the 22nd anniversary of Lindbergh’s landing in Paris). Bands accompanied it through St. Cloud. When the caravan arrived in Melrose, it joined a 40-unit parade. The 28-year-old Gallagher, his wife, Mary, and baby daughter, Kathleen, rode in an open car as F-882 fighter planes did acrobatics overhead. Governor Luther Youngdahl and famed radio commentator Cedric Adams led the ceremonies at the Legion Park. Gallagher retired from the Air Force in 1972 as Vice Wing Commander. In 1984 he received the Jimmy Doolittle Award from the Air Force for his flight. He died in Washington DC on Feb. 24, 1985.

### Air Ace Francis Lent

Francis Lent was a Melrose storekeeper until 1942, when he sold Lent Grocery and enlisted as a cadet in the US Army Air Force. On July 10, 1943, 1st Lieutenant Lent was sent to the South Pacific theatre of war. A month later, he shot down his first Japanese Zero. By October, he had brought the total to 6, qualifying him as an Ace Pilot. By November, he had added 2 more to his score. He credited his success to his trap shooting days in Melrose.

He shot down 8 planes in 4 months to earn the Air Medal (Oak Leaf Cluster), Distinguished Flying Cross and Silver Star.

By Feb. 1944 he was ranked one of the top 11 of the Southwest Pacific’s 5th Air Force fighter aces. The 26-year-old received his Captain’s commission that July.

In 1945, with 11 enemy planes and a few probables to his credit, Lent, who was scheduled to return home to his family and fiancée, volunteered to fly a mission for someone else. The P-51 Mustang, T-Rigor Mortis, was unfamiliar to him, which caused him to crash during an attempted landing in New Guinea. His death was officially announced on June 22, 1945. The memorial service was at St. Boniface Church on July 7, 1945.
